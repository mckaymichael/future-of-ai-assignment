### **Executive Summary**

In this interview, the hosts present a decade-long speculative timeline (2027, 2031, and 2036\) to Ryan, an audio professional, pitching him optimistic, neutral, and pessimistic headlines regarding Artificial Intelligence's impact on the music industry. Ryan expresses deep frustration with AI being used to skip the creative process, noting that low-effort, AI-generated artists are already flooding the market and securing multi-million dollar record deals. However, he remains optimistic about human authenticity. He strongly believes that AI should be relegated to tedious, technical busywork—like de-noising audio, fixing phase issues, or doing taxes—so that humans can continue to focus on the inherently human, capturing-a-moment magic of making music.

He has experienced the gruelling, tedious side of audio production—such as spending two hours manually tuning every vocal note for a singer—and strongly believes AI should eliminate that technical busywork rather than replace the fun, creative parts of making art.

# 2027 Most Likely

2027 most likely – the AI flood and trust fracture

**Headline:** The AI Flood: Playlists Fill Up, Trust Starts to Fracture

**Cell narrative:**

Streaming platforms are flooded with AI-generated tracks and synthetic “artists,” some landing real label deals while human musicians struggle for visibility. Ryan already sees “fully AI-generated personas with AI voices and beats…securing $3 million record deals while real talent is sidelined,” calling the current moment “a temporary kick in the teeth” for artists who spend years on a record. 

By 2027, the most realistic scenario is not full replacement, but **oversaturation**. Generative tools make it trivial to upload thousands of tracks, and creator-economy reports already describe a “post-DAW, post-instrument future” where more people are “creating” than ever, but discovery and differentiation become brutal. 

Ryan frames this as emotionally demotivating: he talks about spending “three years crafting an album only to hear AI instantly produce something passable,” describing it as a “kick in the teeth” that can make artists question whether the grind is worth it. At the same time, he doesn’t see this as the end of human music—more like a painful correction where audiences slowly learn to value authenticity again.

**Assumption:**   
Platforms remain mostly open-upload and recommendation systems are agnostic to human vs AI.

**Second-order effect:**

* **Trust erosion:** Fans begin to question whether the artists they follow are “real,” especially in electronic and pop genres.  
* **New roles:** Curators, human-only labels, and “verified human” badges emerge as value filters.  
* **Inequality:** Top artists and well-branded humans still win; mid-tier and emerging musicians feel the squeeze.

# 2030 Optimistic

2030 optimistic – quarantined AI and human-only spaces

**Headline:** Quarantined AI: Human-Only Spaces Become a Selling Point

**Narrative:**

By 2030, AI music has its own major platforms and clearly labelled sections on streaming apps, while human-only spaces become a premium experience. Ryan “enthusiastically supports” the idea of an AI music platform in the top 3, saying he wants AI music to be “quarantined” and praising platforms like Bandcamp for banning AI uploads. 

In this optimistic path, regulation, platform policy, and audience fatigue converge. After years of AI spam, listeners demand transparency. Major services introduce **AI labels**, filters, and even separate tabs for synthetic content. Indie platforms double down on human-only catalogues as a brand promise.

Ryan’s stance anticipates this: he argues that AI should “do my taxes so I can do this stuff. Not AI to do this stuff, so I can do my taxes,” drawing a hard line between administrative automation and creative replacement. He celebrates Bandcamp’s ban on AI uploads as a model for how platforms can protect human scenes.

**Assumption:**  
 Policy and consumer pressure force platforms to disclose AI use.

**Second-order effects:**

* **New labels & tags:** “Human-performed,” “AI-assisted,” and “fully synthetic” become standard metadata.  
* **Business models:** Human-only festivals, venues, and streaming tiers emerge as premium cultural products.  
* **New roles:** Auditors and certifiers of “human authenticity” become part of the ecosystem.

# 2035 Pessimistic

2035 pessimistic – dead artists on tour

**Headline:** Dead Artists on Tour: When Labels Don’t Tell You They’re Gone

**Narrative:**

In the darkest 2035 scenario, labels quietly continue releasing “new” music and even live-streamed performances from artists who died years earlier, using voice-printing and performance synthesis without disclosure. Ryan calls this “freaky” but says it will “definitely happen someday,” even as he rejects the idea that humans will ever stop playing instruments. 

The tech to clone voices and performance styles is mature and cheap. For catalogue-rich artists, the economic incentive is obvious: infinite “new” releases, tours, and brand deals without the human in the loop. The ethical line is disclosure and consent—and in this pessimistic path, both are routinely ignored.

Ryan’s quote—“creating printed voices to hide an artist’s death is ‘freaky’ but ‘actually going to definitely happen someday’”—anchors this scenario in a practitioner’s intuition rather than pure sci‑fi. At the same time, he insists that “humans will never stop playing physical instruments,” because that would mean erasing “thousands of years of human culture.”

**Assumption:**   
Legal frameworks lag behind generative performance tech; estates and labels hold most power.

**Second-order effects:**

* **Fan backlash:** Grassroots movements demand “living artist only” certifications and boycott synthetic tours.  
* **New roles:** Ethicists, rights managers, and “posthumous performance designers” become recognized professions.  
* **Cultural confusion:** Younger listeners grow up unsure what “live” or “authentic” even means.
